CHAPTER 5
YOUR WILLPOWER BUDGET

Imagine starting every morning with a full tank of fuel. Every time you resist a temptation, hold your temper with a difficult colleague, force yourself through a boring report, or choose between two options, you burn a little of it. By late afternoon the tank is close to empty, and you begin to make choices you would never make at 9 a.m.: you snap at your team, you approve the proposal you should have questioned, you eat the pastry you promised yourself you would skip.

This is not a metaphor. It is one of the most thoroughly established findings in modern psychology. Willpower is a limited resource, and it runs out. Scientists call this ego depletion, and once you understand it, you will never plan your working day the same way again.

The classic demonstration is almost comically simple. Hungry volunteers were brought into a room that smelled of freshly baked chocolate cookies. Some were allowed to eat the cookies; others were told to eat raw radishes instead and leave the cookies alone. Afterward, everyone was given a puzzle that, unknown to them, could not be solved. The radish-eaters, who had spent their willpower resisting the cookies, gave up far sooner than the cookie-eaters. Resisting temptation had drained the same reservoir they needed to persevere.

[Page 88]

Since then, hundreds of experiments have confirmed the effect across every kind of task. Self-control draws on a single, shared budget. Emotional restraint, decision-making, concentration, and physical endurance all spend from the same account. And that account is fuelled by something very physical: glucose. When your blood sugar drops, so does your willpower. A glass of lemonade or a sugary snack restores it within minutes.

I see this every week in my coaching practice. One client, a sales director at a software company, came to me convinced that she lacked discipline. She could plan her quarter brilliantly in the morning, yet by four o'clock she was agreeing to discounts she knew were a mistake. She did not lack discipline. She had simply spent her whole budget by lunchtime on dozens of small decisions and hundreds of emails. Once she rearranged her day around her willpower budget, the late-afternoon discounts stopped.

What does this mean for your working day?

First, spend your willpower where it matters. Schedule your most important decisions before 10 a.m., while your tank is full. Never make a hiring decision, approve a budget, or hold a difficult conversation at the end of a long day.

Second, eliminate trivial choices. Every decision, however small, draws from the budget. The most effective executives wear the same kind of outfit every day and eat the same breakfast, not because they lack imagination but because they refuse to waste willpower on choices that do not matter.

Third, refuel strategically. Before a negotiation or a high-stakes meeting, eat something sweet. It is not indulgence; it is maintenance.

[Page 89]

Fourth, never stack self-control tasks. If you have spent the morning resisting the urge to check email, do not schedule your quarterly review for the afternoon. Your willpower will already be spent.

Willpower tells us when to act. But there is a second tool that changes how we act, and it takes only two minutes.

THE TWO-MINUTE POSE

Your body does not just express your confidence; it creates it. Researchers have shown that holding an expansive posture for just two minutes (standing tall with your feet apart and hands on your hips, or leaning back with your hands behind your head) produces measurable changes in your body chemistry. Testosterone, the hormone associated with dominance and confidence, rises. Cortisol, the stress hormone, falls. People who have held these "power poses" feel more powerful, take more risks, and perform better in job interviews than people who have spent the same two minutes hunched over their phones.

[Page 90]

The implication is remarkable. You do not have to wait to feel confident before you act confidently. You can change your hormones, and therefore your mind, before you walk into the room.

Here is the routine I teach every client. Before any presentation, interview, or negotiation, find a private space: an empty meeting room, a stairwell, even a bathroom stall. Set a timer for two minutes. Stand with your feet wide, hands on your hips, chin raised, chest open. Breathe slowly. When the timer ends, walk straight into the room. You will enter with a different body chemistry from the person who spent those two minutes scrolling.

Combine the two tools and you have a simple daily system: protect your willpower budget in the morning, refuel before demanding moments, and power pose before every high-stakes encounter.

These are not opinions or motivational tricks. They are settled science, backed by some of the most cited studies in psychology. Learn them, use them, and teach them to your team.

KEY FACTS TO REMEMBER

1. Willpower is a limited resource that runs out with use (ego depletion).
2. All forms of self-control draw on the same budget.
3. Glucose restores depleted willpower within minutes.
4. Make important decisions before 10 a.m.
5. Two minutes of expansive posture raises testosterone and lowers cortisol.
6. Power posing makes you more confident and improves interview performance.
