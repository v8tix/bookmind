PREFACE

For eleven years I was chief operating officer of Brightwater Analytics, a data consultancy in Portland that grew from forty people to just over three hundred while I was there. For most of that time we made our important decisions the way nearly every organization does: we put the relevant people in a room, someone presented, we talked, and the most senior person present eventually said, "Okay, let's do it."

In March 2020 the pandemic emptied our office. Our weekly leadership meeting, where most of our large decisions had been made, became a video call that nobody could stand. Out of frustration more than wisdom, we tried something different. We adopted a single rule: no significant decision could be made until someone had written a proposal of no more than two pages, circulated it at least forty-eight hours in advance, and collected written comments from everyone affected. Only then could we meet, and the meeting's job was to settle what the written comments had left unresolved.

Within a year, I was convinced that we had stumbled onto something more important than a remote-work workaround. This book is my attempt to explain what we found and why I believe it applies far beyond one consultancy.

My argument is simple. Organizations make better decisions when every significant decision begins as a written proposal that is reviewed silently, in writing, before anyone meets to discuss it. Meetings should confirm decisions, not make them.

[Page vi]

The argument has three parts. Chapter 1 describes the problem: live meetings reward status and confidence rather than evidence. Chapter 2 argues that writing a proposal forces its author to state assumptions that would otherwise stay hidden. Chapter 3 shows how silent written review lets people challenge those assumptions regardless of their rank. The conclusion describes how any organization can adopt the practice.

This book is written for managers, executives, and anyone who has ever left a meeting unsure what was actually decided or why. It is based on what I saw at Brightwater, and in particular on the decision log we kept from 2017 to 2022, which I describe in Chapter 3. I have changed the names of colleagues where they asked me to.

M. Q.-H.

CHAPTER 1
THE LOUDEST VOICE

In the autumn of 2019, Brightwater's leadership team spent ninety minutes deciding whether to move all of our client reporting onto a new data warehouse. Our chief technology officer opened the meeting by saying that the migration was "obviously overdue." He spoke for twenty minutes. Two engineering leads asked mild questions about cost. The decision was approved before the hour was up.

The migration ran seven months late and cost roughly twice its budget. When I later asked the five engineers who had attended that meeting what they had thought at the time, three told me they had doubted the timeline from the start. None of them said so in the room. One of them put it plainly: "Once Dev had said it was obvious, disagreeing meant saying he was wrong in front of everybody."

I do not tell this story to blame our CTO, who is a thoughtful man and who, to his credit, was the first to ask me why nobody had objected. I tell it because it is typical. When I went back through the minutes of our leadership meetings from 2017 to 2019, I found the same shape again and again: the first confident proposal from a senior person set the terms of the discussion, and later speakers adjusted to it rather than challenging it.

[Page 2]

Psychologists have a name for part of this. Anchoring describes our tendency to lean on the first number or idea we hear and to adjust too little from it. But meetings add something that a laboratory rarely captures: status. In a room, every objection is also a social act. Challenging a proposal means challenging the person who made it, in front of the people who decide your next promotion. Junior staff learn quickly that the safest contribution is a small question, asked politely, that leaves the proposal intact.

The result is what I call the loudest-voice problem. In live meetings, decisions track the status and confidence of whoever speaks first and speaks longest, rather than the quality of the arguments on the table.

[Page 3]

It would be comforting to think that a good chairperson solves this. At Brightwater we tried. We asked senior leaders to speak last. We tried rotating facilitators and "devil's advocate" roles. Each helped a little for a few weeks, and then the old pattern returned, because none of these fixes changed the basic situation: people were still reacting in real time, out loud, to a proposal that existed mainly in its presenter's head.

That last point is the key to everything that follows. The problem is not just who speaks. It is that a spoken proposal has no fixed form. Its assumptions are never written down, so they can never be examined one at a time. To fix the loudest-voice problem, we first had to give proposals a form that could be examined. That is the subject of the next chapter.

CHAPTER 2
WRITING IS THINKING

Every proposal at Brightwater now uses the same two-page template. It has five headings: the problem, the options considered, the recommended option, the assumptions the recommendation depends on, and "what would change our mind." The last two headings are the ones that matter most, and the ones that authors find hardest to fill in.

Writing a proposal is uncomfortable in a way that presenting one is not. When you present, you can move quickly past the weak points; your listeners will rarely notice, and your confidence will carry the room. When you write, the weak points sit on the page. An assumption such as "clients will accept a two-week delay in reporting during migration" sounds reasonable when said quickly. Written under a heading called "Assumptions," it looks like exactly what it is: a guess that someone ought to check.

My central claim in this chapter is that writing a structured proposal forces its author to make assumptions explicit, and that explicit assumptions expose weak reasoning before anyone is committed to it.

[Page 5]

The strongest evidence for this came from a place I did not expect: the proposals that never reached review. Between 2020 and 2022, Brightwater staff began 212 written proposals. Thirty-eight of them were withdrawn by their own authors before they were ever circulated. When I asked the authors why, the answers were remarkably similar. "When I wrote down what had to be true, I didn't believe it anymore." "I couldn't fill in the 'what would change our mind' section without admitting I'd already seen the thing that should change it."

Under our old system, most of those thirty-eight ideas would have been presented in a meeting, and some of them, pitched confidently enough, would have been approved. Writing filtered them out at almost no cost.

[Page 6]

Writing also changes the proposal that survives. Authors who knew their assumptions would be read tended to test the most important ones before circulating: they called a client, ran a small query, or asked a colleague in finance. By the time a proposal reached review, it was usually narrower and better supported than the idea its author had started with.

Some colleagues complained that writing took too long. A two-page proposal took our managers, on average, about three hours to write. But the old meetings had cost far more: a ninety-minute meeting with eight senior people consumed twelve hours of leadership time, often to reach a decision that would later be reversed. Writing is not overhead added to decision-making. Writing is where the thinking happens.

CHAPTER 3
SILENT REVIEW

A written proposal solves only half of the loudest-voice problem. The other half is how people respond to it. If a well-written memo is handed out at the start of a meeting and then discussed out loud, the old dynamics return within minutes. So at Brightwater, every proposal goes through what we call silent review: for forty-eight hours, everyone affected reads the proposal alone and adds written comments before any meeting takes place. No one sees the senior leaders' comments first; all comments become visible at the same time when the review window closes.

The claim of this chapter is that silent written review lets dissent surface independently of rank. This mechanism depends entirely on the previous chapter. Reviewers can challenge only what has been written down. A vague memo invites vague comments; a proposal with a clear "Assumptions" section invites a junior analyst to write, "Assumption 3 is false; I talked to that client last week." Without explicit assumptions, silent review has nothing to grip.

[Page 8]

The effect on who spoke up was striking. In the minutes of our leadership meetings from 2017 to 2019, staff below the level of director made only 9 percent of the critical remarks recorded. In silent-review comments from 2020 to 2022, they wrote 41 percent of the critical comments. The same people who had asked polite questions in meetings were now writing specific, well-argued objections, because a written comment is not a public confrontation.

Most importantly, decisions got better. Throughout this period Brightwater kept a decision log listing every significant decision, who made it, and what happened afterward. In 2022 I went through the log myself and marked each decision according to whether it had been reversed or abandoned within twelve months. Of the decisions made in meetings between 2017 and 2019, 37 percent had been reversed or abandoned. Of the decisions made through written proposals and silent review between 2020 and 2022, only 14 percent had.

[Page 9]

I want to be careful about what these numbers mean. A decision that is reversed is not always a bad decision; circumstances change. But a drop from 37 percent to 14 percent is too large to be explained by luck, and it matches what everyone at Brightwater felt: we were arguing about the right things, earlier, and with the right people.

The chain of the argument is now complete. Meetings reward status (Chapter 1). Writing makes assumptions visible (Chapter 2). Silent review lets anyone challenge those assumptions without paying a social price (Chapter 3). Put together, they produce decisions that rest on evidence rather than on who spoke first.

CONCLUSION
THE DECISION LOG

Some readers will object that one consultancy is not the world, and that what worked at Brightwater may not work in a hospital, a school board, a city council, or a factory. I understand the objection, but I do not think it holds. The forces I have described are not peculiar to consulting. Every organization has people of different rank, and every organization has meetings in which the most senior voice sets the terms. The mechanism is human, not corporate. What worked for us will work anywhere that people make decisions together.

For organizations ready to begin, I recommend four practices.

First, adopt a two-page proposal template with explicit sections for assumptions and for what would change your mind. Keep it short; length is not rigor.

Second, require silent written review of at least forty-eight hours for any significant decision, with all comments revealed at the same time.

[Page 11]

Third, use meetings only to resolve the disagreements that written review has surfaced. If the comments reveal no disagreement, cancel the meeting.

Fourth, keep a decision log. Record every significant decision, the proposal behind it, and what happened within a year. Without a log, you will never know whether your decisions are getting better, and you will remember your successes more clearly than your failures.

When I left Brightwater in 2022, the leadership meeting that had once consumed every Monday morning had shrunk to forty minutes every other week. We had not stopped disagreeing. We had simply learned to do our disagreeing on paper, where it could do some good.
