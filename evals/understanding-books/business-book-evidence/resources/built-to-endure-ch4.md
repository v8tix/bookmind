CHAPTER 4
WALK THE FLOOR

Every enduring company I have studied shares one habit at the top. Its leader leaves the executive floor at least one day a week and works alongside the people who serve customers: driving the routes, staffing the counter, answering the phones. I call this habit Walking the Floor, and it is the single most reliable predictor of long-term success I have found in fifteen years of studying companies.

The lesson of this chapter is simple: leaders who walk the floor every week build companies that outlast and outgrow their competitors.

Consider Tessaly Freight. In 2009 it was a regional trucking company with forty trucks and thin margins. Its new chief executive, Rosalind Achterberg, made a rule for herself: every Tuesday she rode in the cab of a different driver. On her third Tuesday, she watched a driver wait ninety minutes at a loading dock because the dock's booking system double-scheduled arrivals. Nobody in the head office knew. She had the booking system fixed within a month. Today Tessaly runs more than two thousand trucks and is the largest carrier in its region.

[Page 62]

Or consider Pinebrook Bakeries. Its founder, Joaquín Ferrell, worked the 5 a.m. counter shift at one of his shops every Friday, even after the chain grew to sixty locations. Standing behind the counter, he noticed how often customers who lived alone asked whether they could buy half a loaf. Within a year Pinebrook introduced a small-loaf line. Sales per store rose 30 percent, and the chain has since doubled in size.

At Corvane Medical Supply, chief executive Hattie Olumide spent every Thursday afternoon listening in on customer service calls. She heard the same complaint over and over: returning a damaged item took three phone calls and two weeks. She rebuilt the returns process from scratch. Customer retention rose sharply, and in 2018 Corvane was acquired for $1.2 billion.

And at Oakhurst Hotels, the founder cleaned rooms alongside the housekeeping staff one day a week for twenty years. Oakhurst's staff turnover was half the industry average, and its guests were among the most loyal in the business.

[Page 63]

These are not isolated stories. Last year my research team surveyed 120 chief executives of companies on the Meridian Fastest-Growing 500 list. Eighty-three percent of them told us they spend time on the front line at least once a week. When more than four out of five of the fastest-growing companies' leaders share a habit, the conclusion is inescapable: walking the floor drives growth.

I would go further. In fifteen years of research, I have not found a single company whose leader walked the floor every week and then failed. Not one.

Why does it work? Because the truth about a company lives on the front line, and it decays as it travels upward. By the time a problem reaches the executive floor, it has been summarized, softened, and delayed by every layer of management it passes through. A leader who walks the floor hears the truth first-hand, before it has been filtered. Employees see that their leader cares, and they begin to report problems they would otherwise hide. Customers notice the difference, even if they cannot name it.

[Page 64]

Skeptics sometimes tell me that their calendars are too full for this. Rosalind Achterberg was running a company with thin margins and a board demanding results; she found the time. Joaquín Ferrell was opening a new store every month; he found the time. The leaders who cannot find time to walk the floor are, in my experience, the leaders whose companies I eventually stopped studying because they no longer existed.

Listen to the people who work for these leaders and you hear the same thing. A Tessaly dispatcher told me that drivers began calling the head office with problems only after they had seen the chief executive in the passenger seat. "Before that," she said, "nobody upstairs was listening, so why bother?" A Pinebrook shift manager said almost the same words about the Friday counter shift. When employees believe the top of the company is paying attention, they stop hiding bad news, and a company that hears bad news early can fix it before its competitors even notice it.

Some readers ask whether walking the floor can backfire. I have never seen it happen. The only failure I have observed is the leader who does it once, for a photograph, and never returns. Walking the floor works only when it is a habit, not an event.

[Page 65]

The prescription, then, is clear. Block one full day on your calendar every week, starting now. Protect it from every meeting, every investor call, every crisis. Go where your customers are served, and do the work yourself. Do not bring an assistant. Do not announce your visits in advance. Do this every week for the rest of your career, and your company will be among those that endure.

KEY POINTS OF THIS CHAPTER

- Enduring companies share one leadership habit: walking the floor every week.
- Tessaly Freight, Pinebrook Bakeries, Corvane Medical Supply, and Oakhurst Hotels all grew because their leaders walked the floor.
- 83 percent of the fastest-growing companies' CEOs walk the floor weekly.
- No leader who walks the floor every week has ever failed.
- Block one full day every week, for the rest of your career.
